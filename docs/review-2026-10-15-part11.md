# Part 11 (2026-10-15): an outside analysis of the grid and its empty cells

The owner brought an outside analysis of Walton and Krabbe's grid of dialogue types (reference §2.2) and asked:
- Review it critically.
- Given it, and the alternative framework it cites (Cohen, Salas & Riedel 2002), should anything be expanded, modified or revised?

**The analysis claims:**
- The six types are sound, but the grid is not a complete classification.
- Its rows and columns are not parallel dimensions.
- The three empty cells could be filled with advice-seeking, team-building, and relationship management under information asymmetry.
- Later literature adds discovery, examination and adjudication dialogue.
- A cleaner frame would cross three kinds of change (beliefs, actions, affect) with three situations (conflict, shared uncertainty, asymmetric information).

**Short answers**

1. **Mostly sound, with one strong insight.** The rows *are* three kinds of change: what the parties accept as true, what they will do, how they stand toward each other. The book supports this:
   - commitment in persuasion is an opinion, while in negotiation and deliberation it is a commitment to act (p. 108);
   - the quarrel ends in a relationship strengthened or broken (p. 186).

   Cohen, Salas and Riedel group the types the same way; search summaries of their report confirm it.
2. **Four weaknesses** (§1):
   - **It misses Walton's own candidate for its best cell.** Advice-solicitation, including expert consultation, is already in the book: a subtype of information-seeking, defined by seeking advice in order to act (p. 32).
   - **Its critique of the columns is overstated.** Read as Aberdein reads them, they already are conflict, shared (symmetric) open problem, and asymmetric information.
   - **Two of its three fillers have weak standing.** One is its own coinage. The other two labels, attributed to Cohen et al., could not be confirmed.
   - **It ignores a dimension that matters for WAG:** whether a third party is the audience or the judge.
3. **What changes in WAG** (§3):
   - **No new main type.** The six stay the canonical, playable types.
   - **Annotation records three dimensions as well as the type:** the aim, the situation, and any third party.
   - **A new type needs evidence,** as a new scheme does.
   - **The candidates and the later types** are placed in the reference with their status, and mapped onto the book and onto WAG.
   - **For the Kialo gold:** three fields, filled once per discussion and the same for every Kialo discussion. The coding of claims does not change.
4. **Not adopted:**
   - the analysis's completed 3 × 3 table, as a canonical table;
   - its label "relationship management under information asymmetry";
   - rebuilding WAG's types on Cohen et al.'s frame.

## 1. The analysis, claim by claim

| The analysis says | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| The six types faithfully reproduce Walton & Krabbe 1995 | **Holds** | A20 Table 1, checked against the rendered page (part 10 §2) |
| The grid is not exhaustive, and its empty cells are intentional | **Holds**, with a caution. The cells are empty *by definition*: the grid classifies the dialogue's collective goal, and A20 explains why each empty pairing is incoherent. "Not exhaustive" is A20's reading; ND does not say it (reference §2.6). | A20 p. 2 |
| Information-seeking does not aim at agreement | **Holds.** Its goal is to transfer information (ND p. 126; A20 Table 2). The row label "stable agreement" fits it loosely. | Reference §2.7 |
| Eristic aims at an accommodation in the relationship, not at resolving an issue | **Holds** | ND pp. 33–4, 186 |
| Problem A: the rows are different kinds of goal (beliefs, actions, relations), not degrees of one | **Holds; the most useful point.** The book draws exactly this line. | ND p. 108 (opinion against course of action), p. 186 (relationship) |
| Problem B: the columns are not parallel; conflict is relational, the others epistemic | **Overstated.** A20 reads them as: opposed prior commitments; no prior commitments (a shared, symmetric not-knowing); one side knowing what the other does not. That is the conflict / symmetric / asymmetric dimension the analysis itself recommends. That situations overlap in real talk is true, and is why ND treats mixtures. | A20 p. 2; ND chs. 8–9 |
| Cohen, Salas & Riedel (2002) criticise the grid and propose a frame of beliefs, actions and affect | **Partly confirmed.** The report exists (Technical Report 02-1, Cognitive Technologies, 2002). Search summaries confirm that it groups the six types as belief-, action- and affect-oriented, in a 3 × 3 grid. We could not open the report: its hosts were out of reach from this environment. | §2 below |
| Cohen et al. propose guidance/advice/instruction-seeking; team forming/mutual identification/bonding; exhortation/seduction/conversion | **Unconfirmed.** No search result shows these labels. Treat them as attributed, not verified. | §2 below |
| Cell action × asymmetric: advice-seeking or guidance; the strongest candidate | **Right candidate, wrong source, and a missed reason.** The book already has it: advice-solicitation, defined by seeking advice in order to act, with expert consultation as its main kind (pp. 31–2). The grid leaves the cell empty for a reason: the decision is the advice-seeker's alone, not the dialogue's collective goal. So it is information-seeking that serves one party's deliberation; ND says the two are often joined (pp. 163–4). | ND pp. 31–2, 163–4 |
| Cell relationship × open problem: team-building or rapport-building | **Plausible only under a changed definition**, as the analysis itself concedes. Under the grid's definition the cell is incoherent: nothing to accommodate. Read as "the aim is the relationship, without conflict", it fits, but such talk is often not argumentation, which is what WAG is for. | A20 p. 2 |
| Cell relationship × asymmetric: "relationship management under information asymmetry", or exhortation/seduction/conversion | **Weakest, and partly coined.** The first label is the analysis's own. The book treats emotional and allegiance-seeking appeals inside other types: advertisements are near negotiation or persuasion, and infomercials are concealed shifts. "Conversion" changes belief, which is persuasion's aim. | ND pp. 122–5, 206–14 |
| Discovery, examination and adjudication are later types | **Holds.** Discovery: McBurney & Parsons 2001, in Walton's later lists. Examination: Walton 2006 and Dunne et al. 2005. Adjudication: Prakken 2008, *AI and Law* 16(3). Each maps onto the book: the inquiry's discovery stage (pp. 97–8); peirastic and exetastic dialogue (pp. 145–8); the trial (pp. 232–5). | Search results; ND |
| Redefine the rows by kind of change and the columns by situation | **Adopt for annotation, not for the canonical types.** As features recorded alongside the type, the dimensions make annotation more reliable and catch dialogues no type fits. As a replacement for the six, they would cut WAG off from the book that defines the games. | Reference §2.7 |
| The types are dominant orientations, not natural kinds | **Holds.** The book calls them normative models. | ND ch. 1 §9, chs. 8–9 |
| (Not in the analysis) a third party | **Missing, and it matters for WAG.** Kialo's readers are an audience, and the legal preset's moderator is an adjudicator. Walton 1997 made the third party the mark of "debate"; Prakken 2008 builds adjudication on it. | A20 Table 2; Prakken 2008 |
