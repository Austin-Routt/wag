# WAG: the Waltonian Argumentation Game. It is your move. (wag-protocol/2)

You are Bob. You argue AGAINST the claim: you win unless the claim is IN when the game ends. Mode: debate. Round 5 of 20. Scores: Ann 7.0; Bob 20.5;

## How the game works
- Every argument is an instance of an argumentation scheme (Walton): you fill in the scheme's parts with short phrases, and the scheme's sentences (listed under "The schemes") are built from them. Its premises hold until someone challenges or attacks them.
- Each scheme has critical questions. To ATTACK an argument, answer one of its questions against it; to DEFEND one, answer a question in its favour. Either way you back the answer with an argument of a scheme the question allows; the answer itself is filled in for you.
- You may ASK a question about a premise or the inference without answering it, or CHALLENGE a premise ("why should we accept that?"): the argument stops counting until its author answers, with any argument for the premise.
- You may argue FOR or AGAINST any statement on the board, as for the claim, with a scheme whose conclusion can be that statement.
- Or put forward a NEW argument, PASS or RESIGN.
- After every move the Carneades engine labels each statement IN, OUT or UNDECIDED. An argument is in force when all its premises are IN and no exception or undercutter against it is IN. A statement that is at issue (argued for and against) is decided by the strongest argument in force on each side, not by their sum: under PE (preponderance) the stronger side wins and a tie leaves both OUT; under CCE it must also lead by more than 0.5; under BRD, also the other side must weigh under 0.3.
- Make your parts true and relevant to the discussion, in plain words: the other player can challenge any of them. Cite sources when a scheme asks for them.
- The debate is about the claim: pro wins if it is IN when the game ends, con otherwise; the scores do not decide it. The game ends after round 20, when a side resigns, or when a side passes while the verdict is against it.
- Scores are live: they count what stands now, so points for an argument go when it falls.

## The schemes

AS:analogy — Argument from Analogy (truth, weight 0.55), can back an answer to any question
  A claim holds here because it holds in a similar case.
  parts: C = Similar case (case, e.g. "Sweden's carbon tax"); A = Proposition claimed to hold (proposition, e.g. "a carbon tax cuts emissions"), filled in for you when the argument answers a question or is about a statement
  reads: {C} is a similar case; {A} is true in the case of {C}; therefore {A}
  unless (exceptions the other side may argue): The case of {C} differs in relevant ways; {A} is false in a case more on point than {C}
  sources: recommended: the similar case
  typical wording: like, similar, similarly, just as, analogous, analogy, same as, compare, as in, in the case of, precedent

AS:consequences — Argument from Positive Consequences (action, weight 0.65)
  An action should be performed because it brings about good consequences.
  parts: A = Action (action, e.g. "introduce a carbon tax"); G = Good consequence (event, e.g. "cleaner air")
  reads: Doing {A} brings about {G}; {G} is good; therefore {A} should be done
  sources: recommended: a source showing the action brings about the consequence
  typical wording: consequence, consequences, would lead to, will lead to, leads to, result in, results in, benefit, benefits, good outcome, because it would

AS:expert_opinion — Argument from Expert Opinion (truth, weight 0.70), can back an answer to any question
  A claim is plausible because an expert in its field asserts it.
  parts: W = Expert or credible source (entity, e.g. "the IPCC"); D = Field of expertise (domain, e.g. "climate science"); S = Proposition asserted (proposition, e.g. "human activity is warming the planet"), filled in for you when the argument answers a question or is about a statement
  reads: {W} is an expert in {D}; {S} is a matter of {D}; {W} says that {S}; therefore {S}
  also assumed: {W} says that {S}, and this is based on evidence
  unless (exceptions the other side may argue): {W} is not a trustworthy source; Other experts disagree that {S}
  sources: recommended: where the expert asserts it (a citation or a link)
  typical wording: expert, experts, scientist, scientists, according to, study, studies, research, professor, doctor, dr, researchers, ipcc, peer reviewed

AS:negative_consequences — Argument from Negative Consequences (action, weight 0.65)
  An action should not be performed because it brings about bad consequences (the counterpart of positive consequences).
  parts: A = Action (action, e.g. "introduce a carbon tax"); G = Bad consequence (event, e.g. "higher heating bills for poor households")
  reads: Doing {A} brings about {G}; {G} is bad; therefore {A} should not be done
  sources: recommended: a source showing the action brings about the consequence
  typical wording: bad consequence, bad consequences, would cause, would harm, harm, harmful, danger, dangerous, risk, backfire, should not, shouldn't, downside

AS:position_to_know — Argument from Position to Know (truth, weight 0.65), can back an answer to any question
  A claim is plausible because someone in a position to know asserts it.
  parts: W = Knowledgeable source (entity, e.g. "a local resident"); D = Domain of knowledge (domain, e.g. "what happens in the village"); S = Proposition asserted (proposition, e.g. "the road floods every spring"), filled in for you when the argument answers a question or is about a statement
  reads: {W} is in a position to know about {D}; {W} says that {S}; {S} is a matter of {D}; therefore {S}
  unless (exceptions the other side may argue): {W} is dishonest
  sources: recommended: where they assert it (a citation or a link)
  typical wording: witness, saw, eyewitness, insider, told me, reported, was there, first hand, firsthand, knows, in a position to know, official

AS:practical_reasoning — Practical Reasoning (action, weight 0.70)
  An agent should do an action because, in the current circumstances, it brings about a state that realizes a goal, which promotes a value.
  parts: S1 = Current circumstances (state, e.g. "emissions are rising"); A = Action to be performed (action, e.g. "introduce a carbon tax"); S2 = Future state after action (state, e.g. "lower emissions"); G = Goal to be realized (goal, e.g. "a stable climate"); V = Value promoted by the goal (value, e.g. "public health")
  reads: The current situation is {S1}; Doing {A} in the situation {S1} would lead to {S2}; The goal {G} would be achieved in {S2}; Achieving {G} would promote the value {V}; therefore {A} should be done
  also assumed: {V} is a legitimate value; {G} is a worthy goal; {A} is possible
  unless (exceptions the other side may argue): Another action would lead to {S2} more effectively than {A}; Another action would achieve {G} more effectively than {A}; Another action would promote {V} more effectively than {A}; Doing {A} in the situation {S1} would have bad side effects
  typical wording: should, ought to, we must, in order to, so that, goal, aim, achieve, promote, policy, plan to

AS:practical_reasoning_goal — Instrumental Practical Reasoning (action, weight 0.65)
  An agent should do an action because, in the current circumstances, it brings about a state in which the agent's goal is realized (goal-based, without values: Walton 2015; the book's practical inference, 22.1). For an argument that also names the value the goal promotes, use Practical Reasoning.
  parts: S1 = Current circumstances (state, e.g. "emissions are rising"); A = Action to be performed (action, e.g. "introduce a carbon tax"); S2 = Future state after the action (state, e.g. "lower emissions"); G = Goal to be realized (goal, e.g. "meet the 2030 climate target")
  reads: The current situation is {S1}; Doing {A} in the situation {S1} would lead to {S2}; The goal {G} would be achieved in {S2}; therefore {A} should be done
  also assumed: {A} is possible; {G} is possible
  unless (exceptions the other side may argue): Another action would lead to {S2} more effectively than {A}; Another action would achieve {G} more effectively than {A}; Something else would have to happen, or be done, before {A} could achieve {G}; Doing {A} in the situation {S1} would have bad side effects; The goal {G} conflicts with other goals that matter more
  sources: recommended: a source showing the action would bring about the state
  typical wording: in order to, so that, so as to, means to, the way to, to achieve, to reach, our goal, the aim

AS:sign — Argument from Sign (causality, weight 0.60)
  Something is probably true because an observed sign usually indicates it.
  parts: A = Observable sign or indicator (event, e.g. "wet streets"); B = Indicated condition (event, e.g. "it rained"), filled in for you when the argument answers a question or is about a statement
  reads: {A} is observed; {B} is indicated when {A} holds; therefore {B} holds in the current situation
  also assumed: {A} and {B} are strongly correlated
  sources: recommended: where the sign was observed
  typical wording: sign, signs, indicates, indication, suggests, symptom, evidence of, shows that, points to, observed

AS:values — Argument from Values (value, weight 0.60)
  A goal is worth keeping because it promotes a value the agent holds.
  parts: V = Value being invoked (value, e.g. "fairness"); A = Agent or judger (entity, e.g. "most voters"); G = Goal affected (goal, e.g. "equal pay")
  reads: {V} is a positive value for {A}; {V} supports the goal {G} for {A}; therefore {V} is a reason to keep the goal {G}
  typical wording: value, values, freedom, fairness, justice, equality, dignity, important to, matters, principle, right thing
