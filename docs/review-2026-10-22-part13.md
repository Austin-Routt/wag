## 1. What the review found

### The sources define each scheme on its own

Measured on `walton.yml` (116 schemes) on 2026-10-22:
- **Premise-side formulas:** 388 (premises, assumptions, exceptions). Only 10 of them are the conclusion of some specific other scheme.
- **Generic schemes:** 14 conclude a bare proposition, and so can back any statement: expert opinion, position to know, witness testimony, defeasible modus ponens and the like.
- **Conclusions that meet nothing:** of the 102 schemes with a specific conclusion, 91 conclude something that no other scheme uses as a premise, assumption or exception.

So the literature and `walton.yml` say what each scheme is and what to ask of it. They do not say which scheme can answer which question, or how one scheme's conclusion feeds another. The game needs exactly that. Drafting tier 1 found the cases one at a time:
- conclusions that met nothing (`should_be_performed`);
- causal steps that do not chain;
- the `brings_about` premise that no causal step concludes.

### The NASCQ holds fields that no text defines

| Field | In the texts? |
|---|---|
| Formulas, the patterns asked about, the questions | Yes: `walton.yml` and the book, through the catalogue |
| Loci of questions without a formula, the burden of proof, variable types, descriptions | In part |
| `semantic_type`, `thesis_independent`, `base_weight`, `cues_en`, the evidence policy, `answer_variable`, what each answer does (`caes_mapping`), the polarity of answers, backup edges, which game question a book question becomes, the English | No: WAG's own design |

For each game question, the catalogue keeps only its ID, text, locus and pattern; for each scheme, only the game ID and its backup edges. The generator that workstream E promised could not have reproduced the NASCQ from that.

Some reasons for these fields are written down, but scattered across documents. For example, [`game-rules.md`](game-rules.md) says why analogy stopped being thesis-independent. None of them has a status.

### What the plan lacked

1. **A goal for the game.** It had no definition of a playable scheme, and no scope.
2. **A home for the game fields.** They had no place in the catalogue, no authority and no status.
3. **Links across schemes.** It had no rules, no shared vocabulary and no measure of how well the schemes connect.
4. **Which game question each book question becomes.** That was filled for 35 of the catalogue's 222 questions, with no written criterion.
5. **Feedback from play.** Testers' notes point at free text, not at a scheme or question. Model players have no feedback channel. Nothing adds the notes up, and none of them can mark a catalogue item disputed.
6. **Measured verification.** It had one verifier per item and no rubric. Nothing measures how much model checks miss, and every model check so far was by the drafter's own model.
7. **The formula authority.** It still named `walton.yml` as human-verified, after its defects were found and five schemes written by models were added.
8. **The decisions.** The 57 open ones were not sorted.
9. **The working files.** Briefs, source packs, helpers and the private book texts lived only in an ephemeral container.

**Metrics and validators.**
- **What existed:**
  - the cleaning gate;
  - the registry check;
  - the catalogue checker with its copy check;
  - 14 lint rules on the NASCQ;
  - the game tests;
  - `validate.py`;
  - the annotation scoring plan.
- **What was missing:**
  - per-scheme progress into the game;
  - how well the schemes connect;
  - play metrics;
  - agreement between verifiers;
  - one generated status report.

Figures copied by hand into documents had already drifted five times that week.

## 2. Answers given in the discussion

- **Fallacies.** In Walton's theory a fallacy is a bad use of an ordinary scheme, not a scheme of its own. The context already handles it: WAG's presets (`data/contexts.json`) set the proof standard, whether evidence is required, whether a moderator is expected, and the weight an audience gives each scheme. The schemes' own questions handle the particular case, for example whether a person's character is relevant to the issue. A weight of zero makes a move carry nothing without forbidding it.

  Some fallacies are faults of moves and commitments, not misused schemes:
  - begging the question;
  - the loaded question;
  - the straw man;
  - equivocation.

  These need the dialogue's rules (Track D).
- **Shared game questions.** The NASCQ's question nodes already serve several schemes: 9 of its 24 questions are asked of more than one. `CQ:likelihood` is asked of both consequence schemes and of sign, at different formulas. What was missing is not a structure but a decision per book question (an existing node or a new one), made by a written criterion.
- **Families** organize the schemes. They drive no mechanics.
- **Independent agents** have found real faults in every check: 117 findings on tier 1 and 28 on one entry. But checks by the drafter's own model have limits that nobody has measured. Agents and validators check; a structured system, whose reliability is measured, decides how much a model check counts.

## 3. The owner's decisions

1. **The goal.**
   - WAG is to become a complete dialogue system, starting with argumentation-based dialogue.
   - Every argumentation scheme and critical question is to be playable in some game context. The context (the dialogue type, the preset, and the NASCQ's fields and links) decides what a scheme can do there.
   - The literature gives the first playable set and its families. Kialo and other data, and play by people and models, refine and complete it.
2. **The game fields** are decided by logical coherence, consistency and examples of use. Data and play then evaluate, refine and validate them, as they will the hand-written NASCQ.
3. **The formulas.** `walton.yml` is the starting point of WAG's own formula layer, not its authority. Every change is recorded and verified like the rest.
4. **Fallacies get no special system.** A fallacious use is weighed by its context and attacked through its critical questions.
5. **No new "question types".** Each book question names the NASCQ question it becomes.
6. **Families organize.** They are not a mechanism.
7. **Scaling.** Rules and links are written by hand first, on clear examples, coherently and consistently. Data and play refine them and add schemes with rules of their own. The work is kept modular.
8. **Feedback.** People and models testing WAG need a better form of feedback.
9. **Verification** gets a rubric, a sample verified twice, and measured agreement.
10. **Independent agents** work within a defined, structured system.
11. **Open decisions** are sorted and categorized by WAG's larger plans.
12. **A private repository for the development workspace,** `wag-workspace`, restored by a session-start hook in WAG. Tools move into WAG as they become part of the process.

The plan works these out in [`plan.md`](plan.md) §1 and §6, [`process.md`](process.md), and [`catalogue-alignment.md`](catalogue-alignment.md) §3–5.
