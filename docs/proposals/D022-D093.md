Generated by `wag-cli --proposal` from [`D022-D093.yml`](D022-D093.yml); edit that file, not this one. The decisions are in [`decisions.md`](../decisions.md); how proposals work is in [`process.md`](../process.md) §5.

**What changes.**
- **D063, the conclusions.** fear, ad_baculum, pragmatic_alternative and
  appeal_to_vanity_ad_populum conclude should_be_performed(X) (A for
  pragmatic_alternative); danger and threat conclude its negation. Each
  recommends an action to the one addressed, the claim WAG's thesis and
  the consequence and practical-reasoning schemes state as
  should_be_performed(A) (D059, D063). Until now they concluded predicates
  that no consequence or practical-reasoning scheme concluded or used
  (ought_to_bring_about, should_not, not_bring_about, carry_out_action, and
  do_other, which the two forms from alternatives shared), so in the
  engine an appeal to fear for doing A never met an argument from negative
  consequences against A. Walton's account of these forms reads
  them so: the fear, danger and threat appeals are species of argument from
  negative consequences, and so of practical reasoning, and their
  conclusion is a practical ought about the respondent's action (Walton
  2000, pp. 138, 140-143, 173, 191); the conditional threat has the same
  conclusion as the argument from negative consequences (p. 141); the
  argument from alternatives is practical reasoning, and the disjunctive
  threat a special case of it (p. 142). What is particular to each (the
  speaker's threat, the fear, the alternative, the admired group) stays in
  its premises. The old conclusion templates are kept and listed as
  orphans. Their links to the compendium become partial, as D029's, D059's
  and C2's restated conclusions did: the book's second person and its
  hedges (35's "had better", 37's "on balance", which Walton calls
  important, Scare Tactics p. 173) are left to the premises and the
  weights.
- **D060.** threat's premises name what the book's 35 names (Walton, Reed
  and Macagno 2008, p. 333): cited_bad_consequences(X,C), which had lost
  the consequence; position_to_bring_about(C), for position_to_know(X),
  which put the action where the threatened consequence belongs and shared
  its name with the source scheme's position_to_know/2; and assert_bad(X,C),
  whose template now prints C before X (an indexed template): the speaker
  will see to it that C happens if the respondent does X (cf. Walton 2000,
  p. 140, the conditional threat's first premise).
- **D062.** danger concludes about the action, ¬should_be_performed(X), as
  the book's 37 does (2008, p. 334; Walton 2000, p. 173), not about the
  danger Y. occur/2's template is corrected with it.
- **D063, the bridges.** Conclusions that name an agent keep it:
  practical_goal's should_do(P,X), two_person_practical's (now
  should_do(A,Y), D093), sunk_costs_precommitment's should_do(P,A) (D031)
  and waste's ought_to_continue(A,X). They reach the recommendation only
  through a bridge: bridge_from_agent, from should_do(P,A) to
  should_be_performed(A), and bridge_from_continuing, from
  ought_to_continue(P,G) to should_be_performed(A) for the action A that
  continues the effort to realize G. D063's refinement asks for the agent
  and the deliberation to be positively the same, or explicitly mapped: an
  exception nobody raises is presumed not to hold, so an agent nobody
  checked would pass. So each bridge has a premise its arguer supports,
  deliberating_agent(P,A), that it falls to P to decide whether to go
  ahead with A: the agent the case deliberates for (annotation records,
  §3). positions.json types its places, so R14 checks that A is P's action
  in the case record; that the decision is P's is the premise's claim,
  which a critic challenges. D063's text named an exception (the agent is
  not the one deliberating); its refinement asks for the positive claim,
  which makes the exception redundant (a critic who denies it attacks the
  premise), so the bridges have none for it: a departure from the text,
  recorded as D093(e).
  D063 also says that continuing an activity binds to the continuing
  action, not only to its goal: waste concludes that the agent ought to
  continue trying to realize a goal, so bridge_from_continuing takes a
  second premise, continues_trying(A,G), that A would continue the effort
  to realize G, and recommends A. That chooses a means for a goal, as
  practical inference does, so the bridge takes practical reasoning's
  questions for it: realize_more_effectively(P,G,A) and bad_outweighs(A,G)
  as exceptions, possible_bring_about(P,A) as an assumption (D004, D070,
  D088, D089; D093(f)). Neither bridge has a negative form: no scheme of
  the family concludes the negation of should_do or of ought_to_continue.
  Outside the family, from_model (F03) concludes should_do(B,X) too, and
  bridge_from_agent takes it; from_antimodel's ¬should_do(B,X) has no
  bridge until its family is taken up. The bridges enter walton.yml now;
  the game has none of these schemes, and takes them up with their family.
- **D031.** sunk_costs_precommitment, the commitment form of sunk costs, a
  scheme of its own (2008, pp. 135-136 and entry 25, pp. 326-327; Walton
  2002, pp. 488-489): at a time T2 there is a choice between doing A and
  not doing it, and P is committed in advance to A by what P did or
  undertook at an earlier time T1; so P should do A. The book calls it a
  practical form of argument from commitment (2008, p. 135), a hybrid of
  commitment and practical reasoning (Walton 2002, p. 491). It prints no
  questions for it. sunk_costs, WAG's form with the costs already incurred,
  linked partly to 25, stays as it is.
- **D033.** waste gets reassessment_against_continuing(A,X), the book's
  second question for 24 (2008, p. 326): leaving aside past losses that
  cannot be recovered, a reassessment of costs and benefits from now on
  goes against continuing (cf. Walton 2002, pp. 479, 482: past expenditures
  do not justify future ones). An exception, the critic's to make out, as
  D055's outweighing exceptions are.
- **D022.** Two variants of end_and_means_1, whose entry (22.6.1) prints
  both readings in its parentheses (2008, p. 325), as the schemes library

  good(X), so the readings do not meet in the engine: each meets the
  arguments about its own claim. end_and_means_1's link to 22.6.1 becomes
  partial with them, the entry's positive reading.
- **D023.** end_and_means_1 borrows the consequence schemes' question of

### 1. Fix the brakes before the mountain trip

**as an argument**, with `fear`; our own text.

> If you don't get the brakes fixed before the trip, you could crash on the mountain descent. That would be terrible for you, and you ought to avoid it if you can; fixing the brakes is the only way. Get them fixed.

Bindings: X = “getting the brakes fixed before the trip”; Y = “a crash on the mountain descent”.

- You ought to prevent “a crash on the mountain descent” if you can.
- The only way for you to prevent “a crash on the mountain descent” is “getting the brakes fixed before the trip”.
- **So:** Action “getting the brakes fixed before the trip” should be performed.

Pressed with its questions:

- **Challenge** (CQ:premise_acceptable), at the premise “The only way for you to prevent “a crash on the mountain descent” is “getting the brakes fixed before the trip””: You could take the valley road and avoid the descent altogether.
- **Challenge** (CQ:premise_acceptable), at the premise “If you do not bring about “getting the brakes fixed before the trip”, then “a crash on the mountain descent” will occur”: The garage checked them last week; they are worn but within the limits.
