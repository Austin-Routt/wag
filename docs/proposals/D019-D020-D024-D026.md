# Proposal D019, D020, D024, D025, D026, D104, D105, D106, D107, D108: The rule family: exceptions that name what they block, the established rule's actor form, argument from rules in two forms, and the exceptional case as an answer

Generated by `wag-cli --proposal` from [`D019-D020-D024-D026.yml`](D019-D020-D024-D026.yml); edit that file, not this one. The decisions are in [`decisions.md`](../decisions.md); how proposals work is in [`process.md`](../process.md) §5.

**What changes.**
- **D020, D019: defeasible modus ponens.** Its rule premise becomes
  defeasibly_implies(A,B), which walton.yml has, unused, with a template that
  already reads as a defeasible rule ("If A is true then presumably B is also
  true"), in place of implies(A,B), which reads as a strict conditional
  (D020). It gains an exception for the case at hand, rule_excepted(A,B):
  the defeasible rule from A to B has an exception in this case (D019; the
  template is ours). The exception names the rule's antecedent and its
  consequent, so an exception to one rule from A does not undercut another
  rule from A (the lesson of D071 and D097: an exception names what it
  blocks).
  Its questions are derived, not the book's (D019): whether this case is an
  exception is walton/rule_excepted; what backs the rule is the game's
  challenge of the premise defeasibly_implies(A,B), which every premise has,
  so it needs no formula of its own (as D023 derived 33/CQ1-2 to a premise).
  D021 stays open: two models read the book's treatment of the form
  (pp. 364-367) and found an added premise that the rule has no exception
  but no numbered questions; a person has not checked it yet.
- **D024: the established rule's exceptions.** As the schemes library has
  them, with its templates: conflicting_or_overriding_rule(R,C), another
  established rule conflicts with or overrides R in its application to C
  (55.1 CQ2, p. 343); and rule_exception_or_excuse(R,C), an exceptional case
  or an adequate excuse defeats R in its application to C (55.1 CQ3). Each
  names the rule and the conclusion, so it defeats this application of this
  rule and no other. D024's "rule (55.2) keeps its form" is read as: the
  argument from rules takes none of 55.1's exceptions. 55.1's questions apply
  to its two forms only as derived questions, which the catalogue cannot
  carry yet (D023), so the forms have no questions here; a conflicting rule
  or an exceptional case is raised against them by challenging the rule
  premise, or as a rebuttal.
- **D106: one mapping for 55.1's first question.** CQ1 asks whether the rule
  requires acts of a kind that takes in this one. links.json maps it to
  has_conclusion(R,C) (what R requires covers C) and applicable(R) (R governs
  this case), as the catalogue entry does; it mapped applicable(R) alone.
  The proposal's question names has_conclusion(R,C), its first target; the
  link carries both.
- **D025, D107: the actor and action form.** established_rule_actor, beside
  the generic form, as the schemes library has it, with its templates: in
  case C, rule R is established for the agent P and requires the action A as
  an instance of its action types, and obliges P to do A unless an exception
  applies; unless another rule conflicts or overrides, or the case gives P
  an exception or excuse (55.1 CQ2, CQ3); so P must do A. It concludes
  must_do(P,A), the regulative rule's predicate, so the two meet on one
  formula. positions.json types its agent and action places, and the
  regulative rule's (D107), so the actor and action checks (R14) read them.
- **D026: argument from rules in two forms.** rule bound an evaluation E and
  a conduct C in every formula, the book's slash (55.2, p. 343), so each
  instance had to bind both. It becomes two schemes, with templates of our
  own: rule_evaluative, rule_evaluation(A,E) and case(A), so
  evaluation_justified(E); and rule_prescriptive, rule_conduct(A,C) and
  case(A), so conduct_required(C). rule is retired (the design's preferred
  choice): an id whose formulas changed would pass silently wherever only
  the id is read, while a retired id is refused by every check that reads
  it (validate.py, the catalogue check's contrasts, the rule cases). The
  other choice, rule kept as the evaluative form and rule_prescriptive new,
  is for the review to weigh (the design: decide with the review).
- **D104: the exceptional case names its rule and its case.**
  waivable_for_exceptions(R) and case_exception(R,X), presumed
  exception_backed(R,X), so rule_waived(R,X). It had rule_can_waived(X) and
  case_exception(A), the latter as both premise and assumption, concluding
  rule_waived(A): X and A unrelated, and no rule named. The book's 56 ties
  the case of its premises to its conclusion's (p. 344), and its source
  presents it as a reply to the established rule's third question (Walton
  1995, p. 147), which is about that rule and that case. 56/CQ1
  (whether the case is of a recognized kind) and CQ3 (whether comparable
  cases back a borderline one) target the premise case_exception(R,X); CQ2
  (whether, for a case of no recognized kind, evidence shows that the rule
  does not reach it) targets the assumption exception_backed(R,X), presumed
  until it is asked. Templates ours.
- **D105: the regulative rule's addressee and excuse.** The agent A was only
  in the conclusion and the excuse was 0-ary, so one excuse undercut every
  regulative-rule argument, whoever's. As the schemes library has them:
  the premise addressee_of(A,X), that the rule on X addresses A, and the
  exception excuse_or_overriding_duty(A,X), scoped to the agent and the act
  (55.3's rebuttal factor, pp. 343-344); the 0-ary excuse goes. With A in a
  premise, regulative_rule passes the Carneades validator, which it fails
  today. The templates are ours: the library's template for the excuse
  repeats the book's rebuttal factor word for word.
- **D108: from the exceptional case to the established rule's exception.**
  bridge_from_exceptional_case: rule_waived(R,X) and case_of(C,X), so
  rule_exception_or_excuse(R,C). Rule R can be waived for the case X, and
  C is the conclusion about X, so an exceptional case defeats R in its
  application to C. It concludes the exception it answers, as D075's answer
  schemes do, so a claim of an exceptional case answers 55.1 CQ3 by the
  book's 56; 56 stays as the book prints it, and the step between them is
  explicit and can be challenged at either premise. This is the design's
  option (a); option (b), 56 used as an answer concluding the exception
  directly, is not taken.

## The examples, rendered

### 1. A card payment and the plumber's records

**as an argument**, with `defeasible_modus_ponens`; our own text.

> Maria paid the plumber by card, and a card payment, as a rule, leaves a record with the one who is paid; so the plumber's records show her payment.

Bindings: A = “Maria paid the plumber by card”; B = “the plumber's records show Maria's payment”.

- If “Maria paid the plumber by card” is true then presumably “the plumber's records show Maria's payment” is also true.
- Maria paid the plumber by card.
- *Unless:* The defeasible rule from “Maria paid the plumber by card” to “the plumber's records show Maria's payment” has an exception in this case.
- **So:** The plumber's records show Maria's payment.

Pressed with its questions:

- **walton/rule_excepted**, at the exception “The defeasible rule from “Maria paid the plumber by card” to “the plumber's records show Maria's payment” has an exception in this case”: The plumber's card reader was offline that week and stored nothing: this payment falls outside the rule.
- **Challenge** (CQ:premise_acceptable), at the premise “If “Maria paid the plumber by card” is true then presumably “the plumber's records show Maria's payment” is also true”: A card payment leaves a record with the bank, not with every trader: many plumbers keep no books of their own.

*Note:* The exception names the rule's two ends, so the offline reader blocks this step and not another rule from the same fact (that the payment shows on Maria's bank statement, say). The challenge asks what backs the rule, the first of the two derived questions.
