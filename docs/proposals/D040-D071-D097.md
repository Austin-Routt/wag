# Proposal D040, D071, D097: The causal exception names its effect: interference(E1,E2); cause to effect cites its source

Generated by `wag-cli --proposal` from [`D040-D071-D097.yml`](D040-D071-D097.yml); edit that file, not this one. The decisions are in [`decisions.md`](../decisions.md); how proposals work is in [`process.md`](../process.md) §5.

**What changes.** The exception of cause_to_effect and of its modal form,
cause_to_effect_modal, names both the cause and the effect it blocks:
interference(E1,E2), that another causal factor in this case keeps, or would
keep, E1 from bringing about E2, where it was interference(E1) (D071, decided
2026-11-22; D097, decided again 2026-11-27 for the first gold). The book's third
question for the argument from cause to effect (compendium entry 28, p. 328) is
about what in the case at hand could stop the effect from coming about, so the
exception it is mapped to names that effect. The template is new,
interference/2: "Another causal factor in this case keeps, or would keep, E1
from bringing about E2." interference/1, used by nothing else, goes. And
cause_to_effect cites its source, the book's entry 28 (p. 328) and the reference
it prints, Walton's A Pragmatic Theory of Fallacy (1995), pp. 140-141 (D040).

**Why.** A formula with the cause alone is shared by every causal step from
that cause. In Carneades an exception that is in undercuts every argument
that lists it, so an answer that something blocks one effect undercut the
steps to all the others. The second independent review of proposal D055-D059
found it in the engine (2026-11-22, D071). The batch-2 gold had three such
formulas (interference(tax_meat), shared by four steps to different effects;
interference(reduced_meat_consumption), by 13292.180#2's step to anemia and
13292.192#1's step to more crop production; interference(meat_production), by
two), and 13292.182 and .198, answers about anemia, would have undercut the
crop step once causal schemes are played (D086 round 1, G2; D097). ChatGPT's
review of D097 confirmed it in the engine: with interference(E1) a blocker of
one effect disabled the other effect's argument; with the effect named it did
not, and a second argument for the blocked effect from another cause kept it
acceptable.

**The template.** An exception that holds undercuts the step: the effect does
not follow from that cause in this case. So the template says the factor keeps
the cause from bringing about the effect. A factor that only weakens the effect
bears on how reliably the cause brings it about, the first question's matter, not
on the exception. The template is in the present tense with "or would keep" for a
factor that would come with a contemplated cause (producers absorbing a tax), so
one template reads right for both forms.

**What it does not change.** The causal premise causes(E1,E2) is the same, a
tendency, strong or weak, whose strength the first question asks (D036). The
modal form's premises and conclusion are the same (D039). The scheme's other
questions are the same. An interfering factor need not have occurred: one
that would come with a contemplated cause is one; but proposing to add a
safeguard to a policy argues for the amended policy (an action amendment,
OC6), not that the factor is present under the policy as proposed. The
format-1 walton.yml (docs/kialo-batch2/format1/walton.yml), which the format-1
gold and the two annotators' files are checked against, stays frozen.

**Steps that still share a formula.** Two causal steps with the same cause and
the same effect share the exception: in two contexts of one case (the engine
does not know contexts, docs/annotation-records.md §12), in the categorical and
the modal form, or by two routes from the cause to the effect. An answer that
the cause is kept from bringing about that effect undercuts all of them, which
is right when the factor is in the case at hand; steps that must stay apart
need different atoms. A second argument for the effect from another cause is
not touched.

## The examples, rendered

### 1. Untreated sewage and an algal bloom

**as an argument**, with `cause_to_effect`; our own text.

> The plant has been releasing untreated sewage into the river all month, and nutrient-rich sewage causes algal blooms, so the river will have an algal bloom.

Bindings: E1 = “untreated sewage released into the river all month”; E2 = “an algal bloom in the river”.

- An event “untreated sewage released into the river all month” has occurred.
- Event “untreated sewage released into the river all month” causes event “an algal bloom in the river”.
- *Unless:* Another causal factor in this case keeps, or would keep, “untreated sewage released into the river all month” from bringing about “an algal bloom in the river”.
- **So:** An event “an algal bloom in the river” will occur.

Pressed with its questions:

- **28/CQ3**, at the exception “Another causal factor in this case keeps, or would keep, “untreated sewage released into the river all month” from bringing about “an algal bloom in the river””: The river is in spate: the flow carries the nutrients downstream before a bloom can form.
- **28/CQ2**, at the premise “Event “untreated sewage released into the river all month” causes event “an algal bloom in the river””: The plant's own report says only that sewage was released; nobody has measured the nutrient load.

*Note:* The spate blocks this effect of the sewage, a bloom in this stretch of river; it says nothing of the sewage's other effects (the bacteria count at the bathing site, say), which other arguments from the same cause may claim.

### 2. Frost at blossom time

**as an argument**, with `cause_to_effect`; our own text.

> Last night's frost hit the orchard in full blossom, and frost at blossom time kills the blossoms, so this year's apple crop will be small.

Bindings: E1 = “last night's frost on the orchard in full blossom”; E2 = “a small apple crop this year”.

- An event “last night's frost on the orchard in full blossom” has occurred.
- Event “last night's frost on the orchard in full blossom” causes event “a small apple crop this year”.
- *Unless:* Another causal factor in this case keeps, or would keep, “last night's frost on the orchard in full blossom” from bringing about “a small apple crop this year”.
- **So:** An event “a small apple crop this year” will occur.

Pressed with its questions:

- **28/CQ1**, at the premise “Event “last night's frost on the orchard in full blossom” causes event “a small apple crop this year””: Frost kills open blossoms only below minus two degrees, and the orchard's thermometer read minus one.

### 3. A drought, the crops and the fires (one cause, two effects)

**as an argument**, with `cause_to_effect`; our own text.

> The region has had no rain since spring, and a drought ruins the harvest, so the harvest will fail.

Bindings: E1 = “a drought in the region since spring”; E2 = “the region's harvest failing”.

- An event “a drought in the region since spring” has occurred.
- Event “a drought in the region since spring” causes event “the region's harvest failing”.
- *Unless:* Another causal factor in this case keeps, or would keep, “a drought in the region since spring” from bringing about “the region's harvest failing”.
- **So:** An event “the region's harvest failing” will occur.

Pressed with its questions:

- **28/CQ3**, at the exception “Another causal factor in this case keeps, or would keep, “a drought in the region since spring” from bringing about “the region's harvest failing””: The farms are irrigated from the aquifer, which the drought has not touched.

*Note:* D097's point. A second argument from the same cause, that the drought brings wildfires, is not touched by the irrigation: its exception is interference(drought, wildfires), not interference(drought, harvest failing). With the one-place exception, interference(drought), the irrigation answer undercut both.

### 4. A meat tax and the price of meat

**as an argument**, with `cause_to_effect_modal`; our own text.

> A tax on meat may come in, and a tax on a good raises what buyers pay for it, so the price of meat may rise.

Bindings: E1 = “a tax on meat coming in”; E2 = “the price of meat rising”.

- An event “a tax on meat coming in” has happened, or could happen.
- Event “a tax on meat coming in” causes event “the price of meat rising”.
- *Unless:* Another causal factor in this case keeps, or would keep, “a tax on meat coming in” from bringing about “the price of meat rising”.
- **So:** An event “the price of meat rising” has happened, or could happen.

Pressed with its questions:

- **28/CQ3**, at the exception “Another causal factor in this case keeps, or would keep, “a tax on meat coming in” from bringing about “the price of meat rising””: Processors would absorb the tax to keep their market share, so shoppers would see no rise.
- **28/CQ1**, at the premise “Event “a tax on meat coming in” causes event “the price of meat rising””: No food tax on record has raised shelf prices: for food the supposed rule has no tendency behind it at all.

*Note:* The interfering factor, processors absorbing the tax, has not happened: it would come with the cause, so the exception reads 'would keep'. The first question's attack has to deny even a modest tendency, which is all the modal form needs.

### 5. Eating less meat, anemia and crop production (adapted from Kialo 13292.180; the first gold's G2)

**as an argument**, with `cause_to_effect_modal`; our own text.

> If the tax reduces meat consumption, cases of anemia may rise, since less meat means less iron in many diets.

Bindings: E1 = “reduced meat consumption”; E2 = “more anemia cases”.

- An event “reduced meat consumption” has happened, or could happen.
- Event “reduced meat consumption” causes event “more anemia cases”.
- *Unless:* Another causal factor in this case keeps, or would keep, “reduced meat consumption” from bringing about “more anemia cases”.
- **So:** An event “more anemia cases” has happened, or could happen.

Pressed with its questions:

- **28/CQ3**, at the exception “Another causal factor in this case keeps, or would keep, “reduced meat consumption” from bringing about “more anemia cases””: Iron-fortified cereals and supplements would keep a lower meat intake from causing anemia (Kialo 13292.182 and .198 reply in this vein).
- **28/CQ2**, at the premise “Event “reduced meat consumption” causes event “more anemia cases””: The figures come from countries where meat was most people's only source of iron; here it is not.

*Note:* Adapted from 13292.180, which says that meat is the easiest source of iron and that any financial restriction will increase cases of anemia. 13292.192#1 argues from the same cause to a different effect, more crop production. The fortification answer undercuts the anemia step only: its formula is interference(reduced_meat_consumption, more_anemia_cases).

### 6. Ice cream and drowning

**a near miss** of `cause_to_effect`: it is `correlation_to_cause`; our own text.

> Weeks with high ice-cream sales are weeks with more drownings, so ice cream causes drowning.

Bindings: E1 = “high ice-cream sales”; E2 = “more drownings”.

- Events “high ice-cream sales” and “more drownings” are correlated.
- *Presumed:* There exists a theory explaining how event “high ice-cream sales” causes event “more drownings”.
- *Unless:* Some other event causes both “high ice-cream sales” and “more drownings”.
- **So:** Event “high ice-cream sales” causes event “more drownings”.

*Note:* An argument to a causal link from a correlation, not from a link to its effect: its conclusion is causes(E1,E2), the premise cause_to_effect takes as given. (Its own questions ask about a common cause, the hot weather.)

### 7. The tax could hurt poor families (near miss)

**a near miss** of `cause_to_effect_modal`: it is `negative_consequences_modal`; our own text.

> A tax on meat could make food dearer for poor families, so it should not be adopted.

Bindings: A = “governments putting a tax on meat”; G = “food becoming dearer for poor families”.

- The action “governments putting a tax on meat” may bring about “food becoming dearer for poor families”.
- “Food becoming dearer for poor families” is bad.
- *Unless:* The action “governments putting a tax on meat” has good consequences that outweigh “food becoming dearer for poor families”.
- *Unless:* In deciding on action “governments putting a tax on meat”, “food becoming dearer for poor families” is too slight a consequence to count.
- *Unless:* In deciding on action “governments putting a tax on meat”, the harm “food becoming dearer for poor families” counts for less because of whom it would fall on.
- **So:** Action “governments putting a tax on meat” may be unwise to perform because of “food becoming dearer for poor families”.

*Note:* A possible consequence offered against an action: its conclusion is a verdict on the action, carried by its bridge (OC15), not a prediction that the effect may occur. A causal step behind it (the tax may raise prices) is cause_to_effect_modal.
