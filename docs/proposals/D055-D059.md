**The problem (D055).** The book's argument from consequences (33) has four
forms:
- positive (33.1);
- negative (33.2);
- reasoning from negative consequences (33.3);
- the prudential form (33.4).

One list of three questions serves all four. The third asks whether
consequences of the opposite kind should be taken into account. On
2026-10-22 the owner gave 33.1–33.3 that question as an exception,
`other_consequences(A)` ("doing A also has consequences of the opposite kind
that outweigh the ones cited"); 33.4 already had it. Its meaning depends on
the argument it is attached to: bad outweighs good on a positive argument,
good outweighs bad on a negative one. Its formula does not change with the
side, and Carneades treats equal formulas as one statement. So an argument
that the bad consequences of an action outweigh the good also defeats every
negative argument about the same action.

The batch-2 gold shows it. Twelve consequence arguments about `tax_meat`
share the single statement `other_consequences(tax_meat)`: 13292.115 for the
tax, and eleven against. One argument for that statement would knock out all
twelve. The gold's four other positive instances (13292.145, .147, .194 and
.235) argue for other actions, so each has its own statement. They would
share it with any argument about the same action of the other sign.

**The change (option C, chosen by the owner on 2026-10-22).** Each side gets
its own exception, naming the consequence its argument cites:
- **positive consequences (33.1):** `bad_outweighs(A,G)`, "the action A has
  bad consequences that outweigh G";
- **negative consequences (33.2) and reasoning from negative consequences
  (33.3):** `good_outweighs(A,G)` and `good_outweighs(A,B)`;
- **the prudential form (33.4):** `bad_outweighs(X,C)`. It argues for doing
  X to avoid a bad consequence C of not doing it.

33.4's likelihood assumption, `strongly_occur(X)`, has the same fault on one
side: it names the action but not the consequence. Two prudential arguments
for one action that cite different consequences share it, so showing that
one consequence is unlikely defeats the other argument too. It becomes
`strongly_occur(X,C)`. The upstream template had both slots, and the
cleaning recorded the lost argument as a semantic concern.

**Two routes for the third question.**
- **A plain opposite consequence is a rebuttal.** It is a consequence
  argument of the opposite sign, which Carneades weighs against the first,
  as the game already plays it: `CQ:opposite_consequences` for negative
  consequences, `CQ:side_effect` for positive.
- **The exception is the further claim** that the opposite consequences
  *outweigh* the one cited.

The exception is needed because WAG sets weights by scheme and context, not
by content. Without it, nothing lets a player argue that one consequence
matters more. Under the usual proof standard, two opposite consequence
arguments that weigh alike tie and both go out. They weigh alike in the
everyday and equal presets. The other three presets do not name negative
consequences, so it keeps its default weight there while positive
consequences does not. That is an omission: D061, fixed with this proposal
if the owner agrees. Kialo makes the claim outright:
- 13292.127 and 5637.12239 (examples 1 and 6);
- 13292.43 (example 15).

For annotators (guidelines §3), the rule is:
- **A claim that cites a further consequence without comparing it** with one
  already argued is a consequence argument of its sign: a rebuttal. This
  holds however grave it calls the consequence.
- **A claim that compares argues the exception** of the argument it weighs
  against. It compares when it says, in words or by a plain comparative,
  that a consequence matters more or less than the one the other side
  cites. Cues: outweigh, worse, more serious, more important, more
  pressing, not worth, the price of, at the cost of, net benefit, net harm.
- **Code it with Other** unless a source-based scheme fits. Bind A and G as
  in the argument it weighs against. That argument is the Kialo parent if
  the parent cites the outweighed consequence; otherwise the claim is
  re-parented to it.
- **A comparison with a class or a whole side** ("than any harm of high
  meat consumption") gives one instance for each argument of that class on
  the board.
- **A concession plus a weighing** ("true, it cuts emissions, but the jobs
  matter more") gives two instances: the rebuttal and the weighing.
- **A claim that a cited consequence is small, rare or minor,** naming no
  rival consequence, attacks good(G) or bad(G), or the likelihood (33/CQ1).
  It does not attack the exception.
- **A right or value set against a consequence** is not this exception.
  Code it with the value schemes or Other; it is a candidate (D064).
- **A reply that denies a weighing claim** argues the claim's negation.
- **A compound consequence** ("cleaner air and fewer accidents") is split
  into one argument per consequence when a reply weighs only one of them.
- **Bind A to the thesis's action, never to its omission.** A reason for not
  doing A is a negative consequence of A. A harm of not doing A is the
  prudential form (33.4) for A (example 16; D027).

**The limits of the sided exceptions** (all tested in WAG's engine by the
review):
- **A balance claim that names no single consequence** ("all told, the
  costs outweigh the benefits") is one exception per argument it answers.
  One proof leaves the other arguments on that side standing (example 17).
  A side-level balance exception is a candidate (D064).
- **Contrary weighing claims do not meet.** "Starvation outweighs the health
  gain" (against the positive argument) and "the health gain outweighs
  starvation" (against the negative one) are different statements. Both
  can be accepted, and then both arguments fall. Declared contraries are
  not enforced (D053), so the reply that makes an issue is the denial of
  the weighing claim, as the guideline says.
- **No scheme concludes the exceptions,** so a weighing claim is argued with
  Other or a source scheme. It is not tied to the opposite consequence it
  relies on. A weighing scheme would tie them; it is a candidate (D064).

**Why not the alternatives.**
- **Keeping `other_consequences(A)`** (A) keeps the defect.
- **Naming the consequence alone, `other_consequences(A,G)`** (B), fixes it
  only when the two sides cite different consequences. When they cite the
  same one and value it oppositely, they still share the exception. For
  example, legal cannabis means more adults use it, which one side counts
  as good and the other as bad (examples 3 and 7). This was shown in WAG's
  engine by the second review of proposal D001-D028-D029 and again by this
  proposal's review.
- **Dropping the exception** (D), so that the third question is answered
  only by a rebuttal, loses the weighing claim.
- **Option C plus a weighing scheme** would be better for play and
  annotation. It is not needed to make C, and it is recorded as a
  candidate.

**D059, the prudential form's conclusion.** 33.4 concludes `prudential(X)`,
"you ought prudentially to do X", which no other scheme concludes or uses.
So a prudential argument neither supports nor meets any practical claim
about X. It is a reason for doing X, as positive consequences and practical
reasoning are. Its conclusion becomes `should_be_performed(X)`, the game's
practical conclusion, as the owner chose for the slippery slope's base case
and the practical analogies (D029).
- **What the restated conclusion leaves out:** the book's call to
  reconsider, "prudentially" (in one's own interest) and "other things
  being equal".
- **Where they are carried:** in the premises (the agent was considering
  not acting; the consequence is very bad for them), the weight and the
  questions, as "the right thing to do" is in the practical analogies.
- **The match:** 33.4's link to the book becomes partial, as 8.1 and 8.2
  did under D029.

The same reasoning reaches the family's other practical schemes, whose
conclusions meet nothing either: fear, danger, threat, the alternatives,
waste, help, distress and two-person practical reasoning. They are
registered together as D063, not changed here.

## The examples, rendered

Each example binds the scheme's variables to phrases; every formula is then read with `walton.yml`'s templates and the proposal's. Read each line as the game would show it, and ask whether it says something true and sensible.

### 1. A meat tax against unhealthy eating

**as an argument**, with `positive_consequences`; Kialo 13292.115.

> A tax on meat would combat unhealthy meat consumption.

Bindings: A = “governments putting a tax on meat”; G = “less unhealthy meat consumption”.

- The action “governments putting a tax on meat” brings about “less unhealthy meat consumption”.
- “Less unhealthy meat consumption” is good.
- *Unless:* The action “governments putting a tax on meat” has bad consequences that outweigh “less unhealthy meat consumption”.
- **So:** Action “governments putting a tax on meat” should be performed.

Pressed with its questions:

- **33/CQ1**, at the premise “The action “governments putting a tax on meat” brings about “less unhealthy meat consumption””: People who eat a lot of meat buy it whatever it costs; a tax that adds a little to the price changes little for them.
- **33/CQ3**, at the exception “The action “governments putting a tax on meat” has bad consequences that outweigh “less unhealthy meat consumption””: Kialo 13292.127 claims that starvation from reduced access to meat is a worse threat than any harm of high meat consumption, that is, that the tax's bad consequences outweigh this good one.

*Note:* The thesis's action (tax_meat), as the gold binds it. 13292.127's comparison ("a more severe threat than") is the exception's claim; the starvation itself, argued as a negative consequence, would be a rebuttal. Its Kialo parent is 13292.7, so its weighing instance is re-parented to this argument.

### 2. A congestion charge

**as an argument**, with `positive_consequences`; our own text.

> A congestion charge would get traffic in the city centre moving again, so the council should bring one in.

Bindings: A = “the council bringing in a congestion charge”; G = “traffic in the city centre moving again”.

- The action “the council bringing in a congestion charge” brings about “traffic in the city centre moving again”.
- “Traffic in the city centre moving again” is good.
- *Unless:* The action “the council bringing in a congestion charge” has bad consequences that outweigh “traffic in the city centre moving again”.
- **So:** Action “the council bringing in a congestion charge” should be performed.

Pressed with its questions:

- **33/CQ2**, at the premise “The action “the council bringing in a congestion charge” brings about “traffic in the city centre moving again””: The only figures quoted come from a city with a metro on every street; nothing shows the charge would free a centre with one bus line.

### 3. Legal cannabis, for

**as an argument**, with `positive_consequences`; our own text.

> Legalising cannabis would mean more adults use it openly and safely, which is a good thing.

Bindings: A = “legalising cannabis”; G = “more adults using cannabis”.

- The action “legalising cannabis” brings about “more adults using cannabis”.
- “More adults using cannabis” is good.
- *Unless:* The action “legalising cannabis” has bad consequences that outweigh “more adults using cannabis”.
- **So:** Action “legalising cannabis” should be performed.

*Note:* With example 7, both sides cite the same consequence and value it oppositely. Each argument's exception names its own side (bad_outweighs here, good_outweighs there), so they share none; with other_consequences(A,G) they would share one. The value dispute itself is fought over good(G) and bad(G).

### 4. Clean air as the goal

**a near miss** of `positive_consequences`: it is `practical_reasoning2`; our own text.

> The city has set itself the goal of clean air by 2030, and a congestion charge is the way to get there, so it should bring one in.

Bindings: A = “bringing in a congestion charge”; S1 = “the city's air being dirty”; S2 = “the city's air being clean”; G = “clean air by 2030”.

- “The city's air being dirty” is the case in the current circumstances.
- Performing “bringing in a congestion charge” in “the city's air being dirty” would bring about “the city's air being clean”.
- “Clean air by 2030” would be realized in “the city's air being clean”.
- *Presumed:* Action “bringing in a congestion charge” is possible.
- *Presumed:* Action “clean air by 2030” is possible.
- *Unless:* There exists an action that would bring about “the city's air being clean” more effectively than “bringing in a congestion charge”.
- *Unless:* There exists an action that would realize the goal “clean air by 2030” more effectively than “bringing in a congestion charge”.
- *Unless:* Intervening actions are needed after performing “bringing in a congestion charge” to achieve “clean air by 2030”.
- *Unless:* Performing “bringing in a congestion charge” in “the city's air being dirty” would have side-effects which demote some value.
- *Unless:* Another goal is incompatible with “clean air by 2030”.
- **So:** Action “bringing in a congestion charge” should be performed.

*Note:* The action is argued for as the means to a goal the agent has set, not by a consequence judged good; practical reasoning, not positive consequences.

### 5. A meat tax and the poor

**as an argument**, with `negative_consequences`; Kialo 13292.7.

> A sin tax on meat would make food more expensive, despite many people not being able to afford it.

Bindings: A = “governments putting a tax on meat”; G = “food becoming less affordable for the poor”.

- The action “governments putting a tax on meat” brings about “food becoming less affordable for the poor”.
- “Food becoming less affordable for the poor” is bad.
- *Unless:* The action “governments putting a tax on meat” has good consequences that outweigh “food becoming less affordable for the poor”.
- **So:** It is not the case that action “governments putting a tax on meat” should be performed.

Pressed with its questions:

- **33/CQ1**, at the premise “The action “governments putting a tax on meat” brings about “food becoming less affordable for the poor””: Meat is a small part of what poor households spend on food, and cheaper foods would not be taxed.
- **33/CQ3**, at the exception “The action “governments putting a tax on meat” has good consequences that outweigh “food becoming less affordable for the poor””: The health gains of eating less meat, above all among the poor, who suffer most from diet-related disease, outweigh a higher price for one food.

*Note:* A plain counter (the tax cuts emissions) would be a positive consequences argument, a rebuttal; this attack is the further claim that the good outweighs this bad, the exception.

### 6. Abusive relationships and access to abortion

**as an argument**, with `negative_consequences`; Kialo 5637.1866.

> People in physically abusive relationships are likely to stay tethered to their abusers when they cannot end a pregnancy.

Bindings: A = “making abortion services inaccessible”; G = “people in abusive relationships staying tied to their abusers”.

- The action “making abortion services inaccessible” brings about “people in abusive relationships staying tied to their abusers”.
- “People in abusive relationships staying tied to their abusers” is bad.
- *Unless:* The action “making abortion services inaccessible” has good consequences that outweigh “people in abusive relationships staying tied to their abusers”.
- **So:** It is not the case that action “making abortion services inaccessible” should be performed.

Pressed with its questions:

- **33/CQ3**, at the exception “The action “making abortion services inaccessible” has good consequences that outweigh “people in abusive relationships staying tied to their abusers””: Kialo 5637.12239 replies that healthy relationships also last longer when abortion services are inaccessible, and that the benefit of that may outweigh the harm of bad relationships enduring.

*Note:* The Kialo text is condensed. 5637.12239 states the weighing claim outright ("may outweigh"), so it is an argument for good_outweighs, the exception, not only a rebuttal.

### 11. A job abroad, as a goal

**a near miss** of `negative_consequences_reasoning`: it is `practical_reasoning2`; our own text.

> My goal is to keep the children settled, and turning down the job abroad is how I do that, so I should turn it down.

Bindings: A = “my turning down the job abroad”; S1 = “the children being at a school they love”; S2 = “the children staying at that school”; G = “the children being settled”.

- “The children being at a school they love” is the case in the current circumstances.
- Performing “my turning down the job abroad” in “the children being at a school they love” would bring about “the children staying at that school”.
- “The children being settled” would be realized in “the children staying at that school”.
- *Presumed:* Action “my turning down the job abroad” is possible.
- *Presumed:* Action “the children being settled” is possible.
- *Unless:* There exists an action that would bring about “the children staying at that school” more effectively than “my turning down the job abroad”.
- *Unless:* There exists an action that would realize the goal “the children being settled” more effectively than “my turning down the job abroad”.
- *Unless:* Intervening actions are needed after performing “my turning down the job abroad” to achieve “the children being settled”.
- *Unless:* Performing “my turning down the job abroad” in “the children being at a school they love” would have side-effects which demote some value.
- *Unless:* Another goal is incompatible with “the children being settled”.
- **So:** Action “my turning down the job abroad” should be performed.

*Note:* The agent argues from a goal to the means; 33.3 argues from a bad outcome of the action to not doing it.

### 12. Backing up a thesis
