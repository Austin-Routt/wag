# Proposal D055, D059: Sided opposite-consequences exceptions for the consequence family

Generated by `wag-cli --proposal` from [`D055-D059.yml`](D055-D059.yml); edit that file, not this one. The decisions are in [`decisions.md`](../decisions.md); how proposals work is in [`process.md`](../process.md) §5.

**The problem (D055).** The book's argument from consequences (33) has four
forms: positive (33.1), negative (33.2), reasoning from negative consequences
(33.3) and the prudential form (33.4). One list of three questions serves all
four. The third asks whether consequences of the opposite kind should be taken
into account. On 2026-10-22 the owner gave 33.1–33.3 that question as an
exception, `other_consequences(A)` ("doing A also has consequences of the
opposite kind that outweigh the ones cited"); 33.4 already had it. Its meaning
depends on the argument it is attached to (bad outweighs good on a positive
argument; good outweighs bad on a negative one), but its formula does not, and
Carneades treats equal formulas as one statement. So an argument that the bad
consequences of an action outweigh the good also defeats every negative
argument about the same action. In the batch-2 gold, 16 consequence arguments
about `tax_meat` (5 for, 11 against) share the single statement
`other_consequences(tax_meat)`: one argument for it would knock out all 16.

**The change (option C, chosen by the owner on 2026-10-22).** Each side gets
its own exception, naming the consequence its argument cites:
- positive consequences (33.1): `bad_outweighs(A,G)`, "doing A also has bad
  consequences that outweigh G";
- negative consequences (33.2) and reasoning from negative consequences
  (33.3): `good_outweighs(A,G)` and `good_outweighs(A,B)`;
- the prudential form (33.4), which argues for doing X to avoid a bad
  consequence C of not doing it: `bad_outweighs(X,C)`.

A plain opposite consequence is still a rebuttal: a consequence argument of
the opposite sign, which Carneades weighs against the first, as the game
already plays it (`CQ:opposite_consequences`). The exception is for the
further claim that the opposite consequences *outweigh* the one cited. That
claim matters because WAG sets weights by scheme and context, not by content:
two opposite consequence arguments of equal weight tie, and under the usual
proof standard both go out, so without the exception nothing lets a player
argue that one consequence matters more. Kialo makes the claim outright
(13292.127, 5637.12239; examples 1 and 6).

**Why not the alternatives.** Keeping `other_consequences(A)` keeps the
defect. Naming the consequence alone, `other_consequences(A,G)`, fixes it only
when the two sides cite different consequences: when they cite the same one
and value it oppositely (legal cannabis means more people use it, which one
side counts as good and the other as bad), they still share the exception
(examples 3 and 7; shown in WAG's engine by the second review of proposal
D001-D028-D029). Dropping the exception, so that the third question is
answered only by a rebuttal, loses the weighing claim.

**D059, the prudential form's conclusion.** 33.4 concludes `prudential(X)`,
"you ought prudentially to do X", which no other scheme concludes or uses, so
a prudential argument neither supports nor meets any practical claim about X.
It is a reason for doing X, as positive consequences and practical reasoning
are, and its conclusion becomes `should_be_performed(X)`, the game's
practical conclusion, as the owner chose for the slippery slope's base case
and the practical analogies (D029). The book's "prudentially" and "other
things being equal" are carried by the scheme, its weight and its
questions, as "the right thing to do" is in the practical analogies.

**What else this settles, and what it leaves.**
- D053: the positive and negative consequence schemes (33.1, 33.2) become an
  exact polarity pair: their premises flip good/bad (declared contraries),
  their conclusions are contradictories, and their exceptions are a sided flip
  (`bad_outweighs`/`good_outweighs`, same arguments). They can be declared
  once this is made.
- A scan of walton.yml for defeaters shared by arguments of opposite sign
  finds only two: `other_consequences`, fixed here, and ethotic argument's
  assumption `character_is_relevant(S)`, which is neutral (if character is
  irrelevant to S, both the positive and the negative ethotic argument fail,
  rightly).
- Informed, not settled: D033 (whether argument from waste takes a "future
  losses outweigh" defeater, as the critic's exception or the proponent's
  assumption) and sunk costs' exception `future_losses_outweigh(A)`, which are
  comparative claims of the same kind, on one side only; D023 (whether ends
  and means borrows these questions); D009 (the game's question texts: the
  game keeps its rebuttal question and gains the exception question at
  workstream E).
- Not touched: D008 (a modal brings_about), D038 (causal chains backing
  brings_about), D027 (33.3's omission case).
- Found while drafting: walton.yml's threat, the near miss of example 14,
  puts the respondent's action where the threatened consequence belongs in
  two of its premises, so it reads wrongly; registered as D060, not changed
  here.

**What is ours, not the book's:** the two exceptions and their templates (the
book asks whether opposite consequences should be taken into account, and
prints no formula); the reading of "taken into account" as two routes, a
rebuttal and, for a claim that they outweigh, the exception; 33.4's
conclusion in the game's vocabulary; 33.4's second question targeting its
consequence premise (the catalogue gives it no target today).

**What to judge in the renderings:** whether every line says something true
and sensible; the two templates, above all with 33.4, whose cited
consequence is one of *not* acting; whether a plain opposite consequence and
an outweighing claim are told apart in the examples; and whether 33.4's
conclusion still says what the book's does.

### 1. A meat tax against unhealthy eating

**as an argument**, with `positive_consequences`; Kialo 13292.115.

> A tax on meat would combat unhealthy meat consumption.

Bindings: A = “governments putting a tax on meat”; G = “less unhealthy meat consumption”.

- The action “governments putting a tax on meat” brings about “less unhealthy meat consumption”.
- “Less unhealthy meat consumption” is good.
- *Unless:* Doing “governments putting a tax on meat” also has bad consequences that outweigh “less unhealthy meat consumption”.
- **So:** Action “governments putting a tax on meat” should be performed.

Pressed with its questions:

- **33/CQ1**, at the premise “The action “governments putting a tax on meat” brings about “less unhealthy meat consumption””: People who eat a lot of meat buy it whatever it costs; a tax that adds a little to the price changes little for them.
- **33/CQ3**, at the exception “Doing “governments putting a tax on meat” also has bad consequences that outweigh “less unhealthy meat consumption””: Kialo 13292.127 claims that starvation from reduced access to meat is a worse threat than any harm of high meat consumption, that is, that the tax's bad consequences outweigh this good one.

*Note:* The thesis's action (tax_meat), as the gold binds it. 13292.127's comparison is the exception's claim; the starvation itself, argued as a negative consequence, would be a rebuttal.

### 4. Clean air as the goal

**a near miss** of `positive_consequences`: it is `practical_reasoning2`; our own text.

> The city has set itself the goal of clean air by 2030, and a congestion charge is the way to get there, so it should bring one in.

Bindings: A = “bringing in a congestion charge”; S1 = “the city's air being dirty”; S2 = “the city's air being clean”; G = “clean air by 2030”.

- “The city's air being dirty” is the case in the current circumstances.
- Performing “bringing in a congestion charge” in “the city's air being dirty” would bring about “the city's air being clean”.
- “Clean air by 2030” would be realized in “the city's air being clean”.
- *Presumed:* Action “bringing in a congestion charge” is possible.
- *Presumed:* Action “clean air by 2030” is possible.
- *Unless:* There exists an action that would bring about “the city's air being clean” more effectively than “bringing in a congestion charge”.
- *Unless:* There exists an action that would realize the goal “clean air by 2030” more effectively than “bringing in a congestion charge”.
- *Unless:* Intervening actions are needed after performing “bringing in a congestion charge” to achieve “clean air by 2030”.
- *Unless:* Performing “bringing in a congestion charge” in “the city's air being dirty” would have side-effects which demote some value.
- *Unless:* Another goal is incompatible with “clean air by 2030”.
- **So:** Action “bringing in a congestion charge” should be performed.

*Note:* The action is argued for as the means to a goal the agent has set, not by a consequence judged good; practical reasoning, not positive consequences.

### 5. A meat tax and the poor

**as an argument**, with `negative_consequences`; Kialo 13292.7.

> A sin tax on meat would make food more expensive, despite many people not being able to afford it.

Bindings: A = “governments putting a tax on meat”; G = “food becoming less affordable for the poor”.

- The action “governments putting a tax on meat” brings about “food becoming less affordable for the poor”.
- “Food becoming less affordable for the poor” is bad.
- *Unless:* Doing “governments putting a tax on meat” also has good consequences that outweigh “food becoming less affordable for the poor”.
- **So:** It is not the case that action “governments putting a tax on meat” should be performed.

Pressed with its questions:

- **33/CQ1**, at the premise “The action “governments putting a tax on meat” brings about “food becoming less affordable for the poor””: Meat is a small part of what poor households spend on food, and cheaper foods would not be taxed.
- **33/CQ3**, at the exception “Doing “governments putting a tax on meat” also has good consequences that outweigh “food becoming less affordable for the poor””: The health gains of eating less meat, above all among the poor, who suffer most from diet-related disease, outweigh a higher price for one food.

*Note:* A plain counter (the tax cuts emissions) would be a positive consequences argument, a rebuttal; this attack is the further claim that the good outweighs this bad, the exception.
