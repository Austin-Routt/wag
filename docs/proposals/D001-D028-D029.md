# Proposal D001, D028, D029: The analogy family in two-case form

Generated by `wag-cli --proposal` from [`D001-D028-D029.yml`](D001-D028-D029.yml); edit that file, not this one. The decisions are in [`decisions.md`](../decisions.md); how proposals work is in [`process.md`](../process.md) §5.

**What changes.** The book's argument from analogy (7) and its practical
counterparts (8.1, 8.2) use one letter for what holds in both cases: a claim
A in analogy, an action x in the practical forms, each made relative to its
case. walton.yml's analogy cannot do that. Its premise says the target's claim
held in the source case, which is false whenever the two claims differ
(D001). Its practical analogies keep the situation in their conclusions,
carry_out_right(A2,X), so they are sound, but they meet no other scheme: they
neither rebut each other nor support or attack a practical claim (D029).

The proposal gives all three one two-case form:
- a claim (P1, P2) or an action (X1, X2) in each case, joined by a
  correspondence premise;
- similarity between the two cases, as a premise;
- a difference between the cases that tells against what the argument
  concludes, as an exception that names the claim or action: it defeats
  only the argument it is about, and a difference pointing the other way
  does not defeat it.

The book's third question, a case like the source in which the claim
failed, is answered by a counter-analogy: an analogy from that case for the
opposite claim, which rebuts the conclusion and is weighed against it. It
needs no formula of its own (7/CQ3 targets the conclusion), and the
practical analogies are treated the same way.

Analogy concludes the target claim. The practical analogies conclude that the
target action should, or should not, be performed, so that they meet
practical reasoning and the consequence arguments about the same action, and
each other.

**The decisions.** The owner approved D001, D028 and D029 on 2026-10-22 with
the changes of the second independent review, which this version makes, and
adopted D053 in its revised form, with the sided pairing below. D001's
options were A, keep today's formula; B, add a two-case scheme beside
analogy, as part 6 §4 had it, so that old games stay valid; C, replace
analogy's formula, keeping its id, title and links. The owner chose C: one
analogy scheme keeps annotators and the registry from choosing between two
forms of one argument, one of them with a false premise, and old games
replay on the data version they record.

**The binding rule.** P2 says of C2 what P1 says of C1, and X2 is to A2 what
X1 was to A1. The book separates an analogy from the conclusion finally
drawn from it (p. 48): a recommendation drawn from a finding (road pricing
ended the jams there, so London should bring it in) is a chain, analogy to
the finding about the target, then consequences or practical reasoning
(example 9). Bind A1 and A2 as situations ("the 2019 flood", "Southfield
now that its school is threatened") and X1 and X2 as actions, in gerund
phrases ("evacuating the low town a day early"). For an imagined source,
"was" reads as "would be", and the base premise, like analogy's CQ2, is the
audience's considered judgment.

**Analogy or a practical analogy.** A practical analogy cites the source for
a verdict that an action taken there was right or wrong, whatever the word
(right, wrong, a mistake, unethical, should not have been done), and
concludes that verdict on the corresponding action here. Analogy covers
every other claim: findings, predictions, deontic statuses that are not
verdicts on doing something (entitled, obliged, permitted), and evaluations
of things other than actions (examples 5, 10, 18 and 22). This is a rule
for annotation and the catalogue. In play, analogy may conclude any
statement, should-claims included, and can be played as an undercutter;
how far it reaches as an answer is D057, and whether refutation by
parallel argument is analogy played that way or a scheme of its own is
D056.

**What is ours, not the book's:**
- the correspondence premise, which makes the book's shared letter explicit;
  it is challenged like any premise, so it needs no question of its own;
- exceptions that name the claim or action and point one way;
- the practical analogies' exceptions and questions, for which the book
  prints none (D028; the book calls 8 the practical counterpart of 7);
- the practical analogies' conclusions, restated in the game's vocabulary:
  "the right (wrong) thing to do" as "should (not) be performed";
- the five templates.

The book describes practical reasoning from analogy as taking the likeness
of two situations as a reason to act in a similar way (p. 74), which X1 and
X2 express.

**Polarity (D053).** The two practical analogies are an exact
positive/negative pair: their base premises are contraries (right, wrong),
their conclusions contradictories, and their exceptions sided
(difference_against in the positive, difference_for in the negative), a
kind of flip D053 now allows. They share no exception, so when both argue
from the same past situation, a difference raised against one leaves the
other standing (examples 16 and 18). A situation in which the course went
the other way is a practical analogy of the opposite sign, which rebuts
directly (examples 12 and 20, 14 and 21). The engine does not enforce
declared contraries: both base premises can be accepted at once. Analogy
needs no pair: its "(false)" form is a negated claim in the bindings
(example 3).

## Candidates: sketched, not proposed

Two patterns fit neither proposed form (the first review: Kialo 5637.12201
and 2629.18591, and a fortiori examples; the second review found more in
Kialo, enough for the candidates route's threshold). Neither is a scheme of
the book's compendium; the literature treats each as its own pattern: the
argument from the more and the less, and legal interpretation's a
fortiori; Govier's logical analogies, Woods and Hudak's parity of
reasoning, Juthe's refutation by parallel argument. They are sketched only
to test that the proposed vocabulary extends to them without reopening
analogy. Whether to add them is D056, through the candidates route
(process.md §8).

**A fortiori** concludes a bare claim, as analogy does, so it too could
answer questions in the game. Its premise is that the target is a stronger
case for the claim, not a similar one. It shares analogy's exception, a
difference that tells against the claim: its own difference, which favours
the claim, cannot defeat it or an analogy argued beside it. Its negated
form is a negated claim in the bindings, as in analogy (example 28).

**Refutation by parallel argument** concludes that an argument on the
board is undercut, undercut(A) in WAG's engine, so A names an argument,
which walton.yml's formulas never do. In play, analogy can already do
this, bound to the attacked argument's undercutter, so D056 chooses
between analogy played that way and a scheme of its own. A scheme of its
own needs code: the move check would have to bind A to the attacked
argument at the inference locus, and the scheme would be used only on the
answer path.

## The examples, rendered

Each example binds the scheme's variables to phrases; every formula is then read with `walton.yml`'s templates and the proposal's. Read each line as the game would show it, and ask whether it says something true and sensible.

### 1. The tobacco tax and a meat tax

**as an argument**, with `analogy`; Kialo 13292.61.

> A tax for tobacco significantly reduced its consumption.

Bindings: P1 = “taxing tobacco significantly reduced tobacco consumption”; P2 = “taxing meat would significantly reduce meat consumption”; C1 = “a tax on tobacco”; C2 = “a tax on meat”.

- “A tax on tobacco” is similar to “a tax on meat”.
- “Taxing tobacco significantly reduced tobacco consumption” is true in case “a tax on tobacco”.
- In the other case, the counterpart of “taxing tobacco significantly reduced tobacco consumption” is “taxing meat would significantly reduce meat consumption”.
- *Unless:* “A tax on tobacco” and “a tax on meat” differ in a way that tells against “taxing meat would significantly reduce meat consumption”.
- **So:** Taxing meat would significantly reduce meat consumption.

Pressed with its questions:

- **7/CQ1**, at the exception ““A tax on tobacco” and “a tax on meat” differ in a way that tells against “taxing meat would significantly reduce meat consumption””: Tobacco is bought by a minority, often and in small amounts; meat is a staple that most households buy every week, and demand for staples changes little with price.
- **7/CQ2**, at the premise ““Taxing tobacco significantly reduced tobacco consumption” is true in case “a tax on tobacco””: Smoking fell while advertising bans and smoke-free laws came in; was it the tax that cut consumption?
- **7/CQ3**, at the conclusion “Taxing meat would significantly reduce meat consumption”: A tax on sugary drinks, a case much like the tobacco tax, left consumption almost unchanged where it was tried; by the same reasoning, a meat tax would not cut meat consumption.

*Note:* The batch-2 gold codes this claim with the current formula, A bound to the target claim and the source claim kept in a note (audit N12).

### 4. A four-day week, from a software firm to a hospital

**as an argument**, with `analogy`; our own text.

> At a software firm a four-day week left staff happier and output unchanged, so a four-day week at the city hospital will keep patient care just as good.

Bindings: P1 = “staff at the software firm were happier and their weekly output stayed the same”; P2 = “patient care at the city hospital will stay as good under a four-day week”; C1 = “the four-day week at a software firm”; C2 = “a four-day week at the city hospital”.

- “The four-day week at a software firm” is similar to “a four-day week at the city hospital”.
- “Staff at the software firm were happier and their weekly output stayed the same” is true in case “the four-day week at a software firm”.
- In the other case, the counterpart of “staff at the software firm were happier and their weekly output stayed the same” is “patient care at the city hospital will stay as good under a four-day week”.
- *Unless:* “The four-day week at a software firm” and “a four-day week at the city hospital” differ in a way that tells against “patient care at the city hospital will stay as good under a four-day week”.
- **So:** Patient care at the city hospital will stay as good under a four-day week.

Pressed with its questions:

- **Challenge** (CQ:premise_acceptable), at the premise “In the other case, the counterpart of “staff at the software firm were happier and their weekly output stayed the same” is “patient care at the city hospital will stay as good under a four-day week””: The firm's finding is about the weekly output of its own staff; the claim about the hospital is about care that must be given every day of the week. What was found at the firm is not the counterpart of what is claimed for the hospital.

### 5. The violinist and pregnancy

**as an argument**, with `analogy`; Kialo 5637.341.

> Judith Jarvis Thomson argues that people would be outraged if they found themselves plugged to an ailing violinist, obliged to keep them alive for nine months. In cases where biological parents do not choose to create life, the violinist in the thought experiment is equivalent to the foetus.

Bindings: P1 = “one is not obliged to stay plugged into the violinist for nine months”; P2 = “the pregnant person is not obliged to carry the pregnancy to term”; C1 = “being plugged into an ailing violinist without consent”; C2 = “an unchosen pregnancy”.

- “Being plugged into an ailing violinist without consent” is similar to “an unchosen pregnancy”.
- “One is not obliged to stay plugged into the violinist for nine months” is true in case “being plugged into an ailing violinist without consent”.
- In the other case, the counterpart of “one is not obliged to stay plugged into the violinist for nine months” is “the pregnant person is not obliged to carry the pregnancy to term”.
- *Unless:* “Being plugged into an ailing violinist without consent” and “an unchosen pregnancy” differ in a way that tells against “the pregnant person is not obliged to carry the pregnancy to term”.
- **So:** The pregnant person is not obliged to carry the pregnancy to term.

Pressed with its questions:

- **7/CQ1**, at the exception ““Being plugged into an ailing violinist without consent” and “an unchosen pregnancy” differ in a way that tells against “the pregnant person is not obliged to carry the pregnancy to term””: Kialo 5637.11606 replies that the violinist is a stranger while the foetus is the mother's own child, so the two relationships are not alike.
- **Challenge** (CQ:premise_acceptable), at the premise “In the other case, the counterpart of “one is not obliged to stay plugged into the violinist for nine months” is “the pregnant person is not obliged to carry the pregnancy to term””: Kialo 5637.344 replies that being freed from the violinist would not justify killing anyone; ending the pregnancy kills, so what is claimed about it is not the counterpart of being unplugged.

*Note:* A moral analogy, but what it carries over is a deontic status (no obligation), not a verdict that an action was right or wrong, so it is analogy; example 22, a verdict on forcing someone, is a practical analogy. The imagined source is judged, not observed: CQ2's answer is the audience's considered judgment.
