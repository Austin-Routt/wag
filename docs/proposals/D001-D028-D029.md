# Proposal D001, D028, D029: The analogy family in two-case form

Generated by `wag-cli --proposal` from [`D001-D028-D029.yml`](D001-D028-D029.yml); edit that file, not this one. The decisions are in [`decisions.md`](../decisions.md); how proposals work is in [`process.md`](../process.md) §5.

**What changes.** The book's argument from analogy (7) and its practical
counterparts (8.1, 8.2) use one letter for what holds in both cases: a claim
A in analogy, an action x in the practical forms, each made relative to its
case. walton.yml's analogy cannot do that. Its premise says the target's claim
held in the source case, which is false whenever the two claims differ
(D001). Its practical analogies keep the situation in their conclusions,
carry_out_right(A2,X), so they are sound, but they meet no other scheme: they
neither rebut each other nor support or attack a practical claim (D029).

The proposal gives all three one two-case form:
- a claim (P1, P2) or an action (X1, X2) in each case, joined by a
  correspondence premise;
- similarity between the two cases, as a premise;
- a difference that bears on what is carried over, as an exception that
  names the claim or action, so that it defeats only the argument it is
  about;
- for analogy, the book's counter-case question, as an exception that also
  names the claim.

Analogy concludes the target claim. The practical analogies conclude that the
target action should, or should not, be performed, so that they meet
practical reasoning and the consequence arguments about the same action, and
each other.

**D001's options.** A: keep today's formula. B: add a two-case scheme beside
analogy, as part 6 §4 had it, so that old games stay valid. C: replace
analogy's formula, keeping its id, title and links. The owner chose C. One
analogy scheme keeps annotators and the registry from choosing between two
forms of one argument, one of them with a false premise; old games replay on
the data version they record.

**The binding rule.** P2 says of C2 what P1 says of C1, and X2 is to A2 what
X1 was to A1. A recommendation drawn from a finding (road pricing ended the
jams there, so London should bring it in) is a chain: analogy to the finding
about the target, then consequences or practical reasoning (example 9).
Between analogy and the practical analogies, the conclusion decides: a
practical analogy concludes that an action should, or should not, be done
here; analogy concludes any other claim, evaluative or not (examples 5 and
10).

**What is ours, not the book's:**
- the correspondence premise, which makes the book's shared letter explicit;
  it is challenged like any premise, so it needs no question of its own;
- exceptions that name the claim or action;
- the counter-case exception, which keeps walton.yml's design: a case closer
  to the target undercuts; a case merely like the source, the book's CQ3 as
  printed, is a rebuttal;
- the practical analogies' exception and question, for which the book prints
  none (D028: the owner chose the difference exception; the book calls 8 the
  practical counterpart of 7);
- the negative practical analogy's conclusion, which is weaker than the
  book's "wrong" (wrong entails not to be done, not conversely);
- the five templates.

The book's own practical example (p. 74) compares different actions in the
two situations, which the two-case form needs X1 and X2 to express.

**Polarity.** The two practical analogies are an exact positive/negative
pair: their base premises are contraries (right, wrong), their conclusions
contradictories, and their one exception is neutral as to polarity (a
difference that bears on taking X2 here undercuts either verdict). A closer
situation in which the course went the other way is a practical analogy of
the opposite sign, which rebuts directly (examples 12 and 19, 14 and 20); it
needs no exception. Analogy needs no pair: its "(false)" form is a negated
claim in the bindings (example 3).

## Candidates: sketched, not proposed

Two patterns fit neither proposed form (the first review: Kialo 5637.12201
and 2629.18591, and a fortiori examples). Neither is a scheme of the book's
compendium; the literature treats each as its own pattern: the argument
from the more and the less, and legal interpretation's a fortiori;
Govier's logical analogies, Woods and Hudak's parity of reasoning, Juthe's
refutation by parallel argument. They are sketched only to test that the
proposed vocabulary extends to them without reopening analogy. Whether to
add them is D056, through the candidates route (process.md §8).

**A fortiori** concludes a bare claim, as analogy does, so it too could
answer questions in the game. Its premise is that the target is a stronger
case for the claim, not a similar one, and it must not take analogy's
difference exception, since a difference is its whole point; its exception
is some other difference that tells against the claim. Its negated form
is a negated claim in the bindings, as in analogy (example 27).

**Refutation by parallel argument** concludes that an argument on the
board is undercut, undercut(A) in WAG's engine, so it is not an answer to a
question about a statement: it is the scheme for a direct undercutter, or
for the argument locus. Its variable A names a game argument, which
walton.yml's formulas never do, so it belongs to the game's layer more than
to walton.yml.

### 1. The tobacco tax and a meat tax

**as an argument**, with `analogy`; Kialo 13292.61.

> A tax for tobacco significantly reduced its consumption.

Bindings: P1 = “taxing tobacco significantly reduced tobacco consumption”; P2 = “taxing meat would significantly reduce meat consumption”; C1 = “a tax on tobacco”; C2 = “a tax on meat”.

- “A tax on tobacco” is similar to “a tax on meat”.
- “Taxing tobacco significantly reduced tobacco consumption” is true in case “a tax on tobacco”.
- “Taxing tobacco significantly reduced tobacco consumption” is to its own case what “taxing meat would significantly reduce meat consumption” is to the other.
- *Unless:* “A tax on tobacco” and “a tax on meat” differ in a way that bears on “taxing meat would significantly reduce meat consumption”.
- *Unless:* There is a case closer to “a tax on meat” than “a tax on tobacco” is, in which the counterpart of “taxing meat would significantly reduce meat consumption” did not hold.
- **So:** Taxing meat would significantly reduce meat consumption.

Pressed with its questions:

- **7/CQ1**, at the exception ““A tax on tobacco” and “a tax on meat” differ in a way that bears on “taxing meat would significantly reduce meat consumption””: Tobacco is bought by a minority, often and in small amounts; meat is a staple that most households buy every week, and demand for staples changes little with price.
- **7/CQ2**, at the premise ““Taxing tobacco significantly reduced tobacco consumption” is true in case “a tax on tobacco””: Smoking fell while advertising bans and smoke-free laws came in; was it the tax that cut consumption?
- **7/CQ3**, at the exception “There is a case closer to “a tax on meat” than “a tax on tobacco” is, in which the counterpart of “taxing meat would significantly reduce meat consumption” did not hold”: A tax on sugary drinks, a closer case since they too are bought by most households every week, left consumption almost unchanged where it was tried.

*Note:* The batch-2 gold codes this claim with the current formula, A bound to the target claim and the source claim kept in a note (audit N12).
