# Part 10 (2026-10-15): a second check, the grid, advocacy and action, and the book removed

The owner asked for four things:
1. Review everything critically and double-check it before the book is deleted.
2. Record two decisions:
   - **A playable quarrel** is ultimately needed for research, but it comes last.
   - **The focus stays** on argumentation schemes, critical questions, and a gold standard made from Kialo.
3. Answer some questions about the types:
   - Is there a definitive matrix, grid or table of the main dialogue types?
   - Were *advocacy* and *action-producing* dialogue covered, and how do they differ from the main types?
   - Are the main types, such as persuasion, meant to advocate, or to produce action?
4. Then delete the book (option 1 of [part 9 §5](review-2026-10-15-part9.md#5-the-book-in-the-repository)).

**Short answers**

1. **The check.** Two independent reviews went through about 490 claims and page citations against the book, Aberdein's paper and WAG's code. They found 42 problems; I confirmed the main ones against the sources, and all are fixed (§1).
   - **Seven errors.**
     - **Cited pages:** twelve citations of chapter-opening pages were one page early, because of a fault in my page-lookup tool, now fixed. Three more cited the wrong pages for initial situation, evidential priority and the commitment store.
     - **Content:** WAG's premise weights, Walton's necessary-condition schema, and plasticity in deliberation were misstated.
   - **The rest were imprecise or incomplete wording.** Two kinds stand out:
     - places where our own reading was presented as the book's;
     - "Proper in" cells that the book fills but the reference had left empty.
   - **Advocacy was the largest gap,** barely covered before.
2. **The two decisions are recorded:**
   - **The plan's Track D now waits for the Kialo gold** (phase 3). Only two small pieces come earlier, because phases 1 and 2 need them anyway: "proper in" on each scheme, and the guidelines' step 0.
   - **The quarrel is the last step, D2h,** with safeguards ([plan §8](plan.md#8-track-d-dialogue-types)).
3. **The grid** (§2):
   - **No single definitive matrix exists.** The book itself has no table, only a list (p. 31).
   - **The closest is Walton & Krabbe's grid** of initial situation against main goal. It derives the six types.
   - **The descriptive tables** vary between Walton's publications.
4. **Advocacy and action** (§3). Neither is a seventh type:
   - **Advocacy is a stance.** It is built into persuasion and negotiation, and out of place in inquiry and information-seeking.
   - **"Action-producing" is a goal.** It is the grid's middle row: negotiation and deliberation.
   - **Persuasion advocates, but does not produce action.** Its product is acceptance of a proposition, though that proposition can be about whether to act (p. 229). Kialo's policy debates are exactly that.
5. **The book is deleted** from the repository (§4). It is still in the history; removing it from there is the owner's decision.

**The main imprecisions corrected.**
- **Overstatements:**
  - **"Every game is persuasion"** now reads "every game with a central claim". The points modes match no type.
  - **Inquiry:** retraction is minimised, ideally eliminated, and a very serious step (p. 70). It was described as excluded.
  - **Persuasion's collective goal** is to test the arguments on both sides (p. 37). Only the critical discussion must resolve the conflict (p. 48). It was given as "resolve the conflict".
  - **The "six, eight, six" story** is not in the sources. Walton had classed debate as mixed in 1982 and 1989, and still listed it in his 1997 table. The lists differ by presentation, not by a change of view.
- **Our reading presented as the book's:**
  - **Kialo** is now "persuasion about whether an action is advisable" (p. 229), not "a persuasion–deliberation hybrid". The book makes a hybrid only of deliberation where basic goals conflict.
  - **"Advocacy is a stance" and "action-producing is a goal"** are marked as WAG's reading. The book names action-producing dialogue as a model, and leaves advocacy open.
  - **Appeals to fear in deliberation** are marked as our extrapolation from caution about harm (tutiorism).
- **Shifts:**
  - **Internal shifts** stay within one type; a shift of issue is one kind of them.
  - **Displacement** is added: the second dialogue blocks the first.
  - **Judged afterwards:** a shift is judged retrospectively, but is not always visible only afterwards.
  - **Probing an expert** is "highly constructive", not merely allowable.
  - **Plea bargaining** gets no verdict in the book.
  - **Political debate** also embeds deliberation and information-seeking.
- **Details in §5:**
  - enthymemes, after Walton & Krabbe;
  - probative relevance, which includes casting doubt;
  - topical relevance, a different notion from dialectical relevance, not a narrower one;
  - the four stages common to every type;
  - the quarrel's turn-taking and its two possible endings;
  - the three ways out of deadlock;
  - advertising;
  - the fallacy of many questions, which depends on what has been conceded.

**What the book says that the reference had left out:**
- **Ad hominem:**
  - it suits the quarrel;
  - it can be reasonable in a critical discussion;
  - it is fallacious in scientific inquiry (pp. 261–2, 270).
- **Circumstantial ad hominem:** generally sound in negotiation (p. 121).
- **The argument from ignorance:** often appropriate in political and moral persuasion (p. 270).
- **A loaded question:** legitimate in cross-examination of someone who has admitted its presupposition (p. 264).
- **Popular opinion and pity:** not wrong in principle (p. 22). Pity is proper at sentencing (p. 234).
- **Suppressed evidence:** a new row. An advocate owes no evidence for the other side; a consulted expert owes completeness (pp. 230–2).

## 2. Is there a definitive matrix?

**No single one, and not in the book.** *The New Dialectic* lists the six main types and their subtypes (p. 31) and describes each in chapters 1–7. It has no table of them; its only figures are of other things. The tables in circulation come from Walton's other works, as Aberdein's survey reproduces them:

| Table | What it does | Source | In the reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The grid** | **Derives** the six main types from two factors: the initial situation (a conflict; an open problem; an unsatisfactory spread of information) and the main goal (a stable agreement; a practical settlement; a provisional accommodation). Six cells are filled, three are empty. | Walton & Krabbe 1995, p. 80 | [§2.2](dialogue-types.md#22-why-six-the-grid) |
| **The descriptive table** | **Describes** each type: initial situation, each party's goal, the collective goal, the benefit. It has eight rows. | Walton 1997 (A20 points to similar accounts from 1990 and 1995) | [§2.3](dialogue-types.md#23-the-descriptive-table-walton-1997-via-a20-table-2) |
| **Aberdein's own matrix** | Six types **as they occur in mathematics**: initial situation, main goal, and each side's goal. It has no eristic, and splits information-seeking into pedagogical and "oracular". | Aberdein 2007, adapted in A20 Table 3 | §2.3, after the descriptive table |

**Aberdein's paper, checked against its rendered pages:**
- **Table 1 is a true matrix,** the grid above. WAG's copy matches it cell for cell.
- **Table 2 is the list of eight,** exactly as the reference gives it:
  1. persuasion;
  2. inquiry;
  3. deliberation;
  4. negotiation;
  5. information-seeking;
  6. quarrel (eristic);
  7. debate;
  8. pedagogical.

  It has five columns: the type, the initial situation, each party's goal, the collective goal, and the benefits. The source is Walton, "How can logic best be applied to arguments?", *Logic Journal of the IGPL* 5 (1997), p. 605. One cell, the benefit of pedagogical dialogue, is printed as "reserve transfer", which is unclear.
- **Table 3 is a second matrix, Aberdein's own.** The reference had used it only for "oracular" information-seeking; it is now given in full.

**Which one WAG uses:**
- **The grid** is the closest thing to a definitive matrix, because it explains *why* there are six main types.
- **WAG's working definition** is the six main types of the book, placed by the grid and described from the book itself ([reference §2.6, §4](dialogue-types.md#26-is-there-a-definitive-matrix-and-is-the-list-closed)).
- **Later lists by Walton and others** vary again, for example by adding a "discovery" dialogue. We have not reviewed them.

## 3. Advocacy and action

**Were they covered?**
- **Action-producing dialogue:** one line in the reference. Now it has a full row in [§2.4](dialogue-types.md#24-other-labels-and-how-nd-treats-them).
- **Advocacy:** only as the bias ad hominem and "advocacy advertising". That was a real gap, now filled in §2.4 and [§2.5](dialogue-types.md#25-advocacy-and-action-what-each-main-type-is-for).

**What the book says about advocacy** (pp. 121–5, 209, 215–7, 227–32):
- **Advocacy is normal in persuasion.** Each party argues its own view as strongly as the rules allow, so each side has a bias. That bias is acceptable unless it is pushed so far that it destroys the openness and balance the dialogue needs (p. 122).
- **A different kind of advocacy is interest-based:** arguing for a stake one has, which is bargaining. Doing so covertly while claiming to discuss is what the bias ad hominem alleges (pp. 121–2).
- **A sales pitch is "some kind of advocacy dialogue"** (p. 217). Analysed closely, it is a mixture: information-seeking, then persuasion, then negotiation, with the buyers possibly deliberating (pp. 227–32).
- **Advertisements split two ways.** A commercial advertisement is like an offer in a negotiation; an advocacy advertisement is an argument in a persuasion dialogue (pp. 123–5).
- **Advocacy limits one's duties.** An advocate need not present the other side's evidence. A consulted expert, or an inquiry, owes completeness (pp. 230–2).
- **The book leaves advocacy open.** It says bias and advocacy remain to be investigated (p. 122).

**What the book says about action-producing dialogue** (pp. 199–200, 223):
- **The phrase is not defined in the book.** "Action-producing" is one of five models that Walton found mixed in the Canadian Question Period (after Walton 1992), because one of its stated aims is to press the government to act.
- **"Action-directed" names deliberation in practice.** The bicycle-path exchange about which road to take is called action-directed, then a deliberation. Question Period is said to be, in part, an action-directed deliberation.

**Do the main types advocate, or produce action?** The key passage is p. 108:
- **In negotiation and deliberation, commitment is to a course of action.**
- **In persuasion, commitment is an opinion:** accepting a proposition as true.

So, type by type:

| Type | Advocates? | Produces action? |
|---|---|---|
| Persuasion | **Yes,** by design | **No:** acceptance of a thesis. But the thesis may be that an action is advisable (p. 229), as in Kialo's policy debates. |
| Negotiation | **Yes,** for interests | **Yes:** a deal is a set of pledges |
| Deliberation | **Partly:** proposals, with a shared goal | **Yes:** a decision to act, held tentatively |
| Inquiry | **No** | **No:** knowledge |
| Information-seeking | **Not the answerer** | **Indirectly:** it serves the questioner's task |
| Eristic | **Extremely** | **No:** catharsis |

**What this means for the Kialo gold: nothing changes in the coding of claims.**
- **The setting:** a Kialo discussion is advocacy within a persuasion dialogue, usually about a practical thesis.
- **The type:** guidelines step 0 records it as persuasion with a practical thesis.
- **The other side's evidence:** the sides are not expected to present it, so leaving it out is not a fault in itself.
- **For other corpora,** the reference's data model has an optional per-party `advocates` field: a point of view, an interest, or none.

## 4. The book removed

- `docs/The New Dialectic.pdf` is deleted from the repository in this round's commit.
- **It is still in the history:** commit f891090, and in any clone or fork.
